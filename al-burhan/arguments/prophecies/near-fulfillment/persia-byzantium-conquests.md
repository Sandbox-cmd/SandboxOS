# Prophecy: the fall of Khosrau's Persia and the spending of the two empires' treasures

Chart step: 4 (Muhammad was a true prophet), fulfilled prophecy.
Category: arguments/prophecies/near-fulfillment
Strength: weak

## The claim

Hadiths attributed to the Prophet, spoken when the Muslims were a small Arabian power, predicted that the Sasanian line of Khosrau would end for good, and that Muslims would seize and spend the treasures of both Persia and Byzantium. Within a generation the Sasanian empire was annihilated (Yazdegerd III killed 651 CE, no successor ever restored) and its treasuries, and those of Byzantine Syria and Egypt, were distributed by the caliphate.

## Primary texts

- "When Khosrau perishes, there will be no Khosrau after him, and when Caesar perishes, there will be no Caesar after him. By Him in Whose Hand is my soul, you will spend their treasures in the cause of Allah." — Sahih al-Bukhari (approx. 3120 and 3618) and Sahih Muslim (approx. 2918-2919), narrated by Abu Hurayrah; a version via Jabir ibn Samurah — verify numbers and narrators.
- The hadith of Adi ibn Hatim: the Prophet, at a time of visible weakness, told him a woman would travel alone from al-Hirah to the Ka'bah fearing none but Allah, and "if you live long, you will see the treasures of Khosrau opened"; Adi says, "I was among those who opened the treasures of Khosrau." — Sahih al-Bukhari (approx. 3595) — verify.
- Related: the Prophet's letter to Khosrau II and the report that Khosrau tore it, with the Prophet's response that his kingdom would be torn — Bukhari (approx. 64 or 4424) — verify, and note this version's wording varies.

## The argument

1. In the Prophet's lifetime the Sasanians and Byzantines were the two superpowers; Arabs were clients at their margins. Medina in the late 620s was poor — the Adi ibn Hatim hadith and the Khandaq-era reports are explicitly framed against this weakness.
2. The prediction is doubly specific: not merely "you will defeat them," but (a) the Persian monarchy will end permanently — "no Khosrau after him" — while the wording about Caesar was understood even by classical commentators as asymmetric (Byzantium lost Syria and the Levant forever but persisted in Constantinople), and (b) the treasuries of both will be spent by the believers.
3. Fulfillment: Qadisiyyah (636), the fall of Ctesiphon and seizure of its treasury (637), Nihawand (642), Yazdegerd III murdered at Merv (651). No Sasanian "Khosrau" ever ruled again. Byzantine Syria, Palestine and Egypt, with their wealth, fell between 634 and 642, and the spoils demonstrably flowed through the caliphal treasury. Adi ibn Hatim (d. ~687) lived to confirm his own narration.
4. Therefore the texts, if genuinely spoken before 632, predicted a wildly improbable geopolitical outcome correctly and in detail.

## Objections and replies

- **The hadith collections postdate the conquests by two centuries — this is the serious one.** Bukhari died 870 and Muslim 875; even the earliest substantial written collections (Malik's Muwatta, Ma'mar ibn Rashid's Jami' preserved in Abd al-Razzaq, mid-to-late 8th century) were compiled a full century after Ctesiphon fell in 637. Unlike the Quran, whose text was fixed early, hadith circulated orally and in private notes through the very period in which the "predicted" events happened. The default critical assumption for any prophecy transmitted this way is vaticinium ex eventu: the community that had just conquered Persia had every motive, conscious or not, to crystallize sayings in which the Prophet foresaw it. Honest reply, stated carefully: (a) Isnad-cum-matn analysis (the method associated with Schacht's critics and refiners — Motzki, and differently Juynboll) can sometimes trace a tradition's common link to the generation of the Successors, i.e., late 7th or early 8th century. Even in the best case, that is still after 651, so documentary methods cannot establish pre-conquest utterance for these hadiths. This must be conceded plainly: the argument cannot be proven on skeptical ground rules, and anyone who claims otherwise is overselling. (b) What can be argued is internal evidence against simple fabrication: a forger writing after the conquests, tailoring a prophecy to known events, would not naturally produce "no Caesar after him" — a clause that looked false on its face, since emperors continued to reign in Constantinople for eight more centuries, and which classical scholars (e.g., al-Shafi'i and later commentators — verify attribution) had to gloss as "no Caesar in Syria/Iraq" or "no Caesar with his former dominion." Prophecies invented after the fact tend to fit the facts; this one needed apologetic help from the start, which is modest evidence of an origin before the outcome was fully known (e.g., during the wars, when both empires' total fall could be expected symmetrically). This is a real but subtle point, and it dates the core saying early rather than to the Prophet specifically. (c) The Adi ibn Hatim narration is framed as personal testimony with a built-in verification claim, and companions' public first-person claims were in principle checkable by contemporaries — but we receive even this through later compilers. Net: the reply narrows the gap; it does not close it. Grade accordingly.
- **Expansionist ambition was already in the air; the "prediction" steered its own fulfillment.** Hadiths like these functioned as marching orders: believers who expected Khosrau's treasures went and took them. Reply: partly fair for the treasure clause, which is of the self-fulfilling type (see the conquest-of-Mecca file). It is less fair for "no Khosrau after him": the permanent extinction of a four-century dynasty was not in the conquerors' gift to guarantee — restorations were the norm (the Sasanids themselves had survived Heraclius' devastation of 627-628), Yazdegerd's heirs sought Tang Chinese help (verify Peroz III at the Tang court), and yet no restoration ever came. But this stronger clause is exactly the one most vulnerable to the dating objection above, since its truth only became fully apparent decades later, giving ample time for the wording to sharpen in transmission.
- **Persia in the 630s was visibly collapsing anyway.** After Heraclius' victory, the murder of Khosrau II (628), a plague, and roughly a dozen rulers in four years, a contemporary could rate Sasanian prospects poorly. Reply: true, but this cuts both ways — the traditional occasions for these sayings (letter to Khosrau ~628, Khandaq ~627, Adi's conversion ~630) sit before or during that chaos, and no contemporary predicted that the beneficiary would be Arabia, or that the fall would be permanent. Byzantium looked ascendant in 630, yet the hadith has the believers spending Caesar's treasure too. The reply has force only if the early dating holds, which returns to the main objection.
- **Selection effect.** The tradition remembers hits; failed or embarrassing predictions attributed to the Prophet would be less likely to survive canonical sifting (graded weak or excluded precisely because later scholars doubted them — and matn criticism sometimes used conflict with known history as a criterion). Reply: concede this openly; it means canonical-prophecy arguments are cumulative-case material at best. The partial counterexample is again "no Caesar after him": an apparently problematic clause that survived in the most rigorous collections.
