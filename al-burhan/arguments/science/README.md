# Science arguments (i'jaz 'ilmi)

Chart step: 5 (the Quran shows signs of a superhuman source).
Category: arguments/science
Strength: use with extreme caution — this is the weakest branch of the chart.

## The argument form

Every file in this folder instantiates one schema:

1. The text states X.
2. X was unknowable in 7th-century Arabia.
3. X is true (settled modern science).
4. Therefore the text's knowledge of X points to a source beyond its human environment.

The form is valid. The problem is almost always premise 1 or premise 2.

## Why the genre is attractive

- It promises empirical, checkable evidence rather than appeals to literary taste
  or historical testimony, so it travels well to audiences with no Arabic and no
  trust in Islamic sources.
- Science carries cultural authority; a match between scripture and science
  borrows that authority.
- It is the staple of modern dawah media (Maurice Bucaille's *The Bible, the
  Qur'an and Science*, 1976; the "Scientific Miracles" conferences and the
  Commission on Scientific Signs in Jeddah; Zakir Naik and iERA-style
  presentations). Its ubiquity is exactly why this folder must be the honest
  version: our readers will have seen the hype and the takedowns of the hype.

## The three standard rebuttals

Every file must answer these three, explicitly, before it can be used.

### 1. Post-hoc reinterpretation

The scientific reading is announced only after the discovery, never before it.
No one read 51:47 and predicted cosmic expansion before Hubble; the expansion
reading appears in tafsir and dawah literature only afterwards. A text that can
be made to agree with whatever science currently says — and that was read
differently for thirteen centuries — is not predicting; it is being retrofitted.
The retrofit also silently absorbs falsification: when a scientific consensus
shifts, the verse's "real meaning" shifts with it (geocentric readings of the
sun "running to a resting place", 36:38, were standard in classical tafsir and
are now quietly dropped). A claim that cannot lose is not evidence.

The only defense is to show that the natural, classical, pre-modern reading of
the verse already asserts X. If classical mufassirun did not see X in the verse,
the burden is on us to explain why the "plain meaning" was invisible to native
speakers for a millennium.

### 2. Elastic translation

The arguments usually turn on a single Arabic word rendered with a conveniently
modern gloss: musi'un as "expanding (spacetime)", 'alaqah as "leech-like
clinging embryo", ratq/fatq as "singularity/big bang", barzakh as "halocline".
Classical lexicons (Lisan al-'Arab, Taj al-'Arus, al-Raghib's Mufradat) and
classical tafsir usually show a wider or different semantic range, and the
apologetic translation picks the one sense that matches the science — sometimes
a sense with no classical attestation at all. Translation choices made after the
discovery, in order to match the discovery, cannot then count as evidence of the
discovery.

The defense must show the modern gloss is within the word's attested classical
range *and* is the most natural reading in context, not merely a permissible
stretch.

### 3. Precedent in accessible traditions

Premise 2 fails if X, or a close ancestor of X, circulated in traditions that
7th-century Arabia could plausibly access: Greek science (via Syriac
translations and the Jundishapur/Nisibis school milieu), Jewish and Christian
scripture and midrash, Mesopotamian and Egyptian cosmogony surviving in regional
folklore, or plain observation available to farmers, midwives, and sailors.
Mecca and Medina sat on caravan routes; the Quran itself records opponents
saying "tales of the ancients" (asatir al-awwalin, e.g. 25:5), so the milieu
was not sealed. A match with Galen or with the Enuma Elish is evidence of a
shared intellectual environment, not of revelation — on this argument form. (It
may still be theologically unproblematic; it just cannot carry step 5 of the
chart.)

## The methodological test

A candidate verse enters the chart only if it passes all three:

(a) **Classical reading.** The natural classical reading already says X. Check
    at minimum al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir, and a classical lexicon. If
    the classical commentators read the verse as something else, the verse
    fails, whatever modern translations say.

(b) **No accessible precedent.** X was not available in any tradition plausibly
    accessible to 7th-century Arabia: Greek/Syriac science, Jewish/Christian
    texts and lore, Near Eastern cosmogonies, or common empirical knowledge of
    the region's trades.

(c) **Settled science.** X is settled, textbook science — not a speculative
    frontier (cosmological models change) and not a pop-science simplification.

Most famous examples fail (a) or (b). Of the four worked in this folder, none
passes all three cleanly; the strongest (51:47) fails (a). That result is
itself useful: it tells us the chart's step 5 should not lean on this folder.

## Muslim scholarly criticism of the genre

Overreach here is not only a skeptics' complaint. Note for the verification
list: significant Muslim voices have warned against tafsir 'ilmi, including
(reportedly) al-Shatibi in al-Muwafaqat (14th c.) against reading the Quran as
a science book; Rashid Rida's reservations in Tafsir al-Manar; and modern
academic critiques by Muslim scholars (e.g. writings associated with Ziauddin
Sardar, Nidhal Guessoum's *Islam's Quantum Question*, 2011). Amin al-Khuli's
school in Egypt also rejected scientific exegesis on literary-method grounds.
Exact positions and citations must be verified before use, but the point
stands: a Muslim reader can reject i'jaz 'ilmi without rejecting Islam, and
our chart should not make step 5 hostage to it.
