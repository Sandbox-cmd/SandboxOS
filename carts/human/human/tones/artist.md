# artist

The academic art register: artist statements, curatorial statements, wall text, and catalogue essays. The language artists and curators write in, done at its best. The evidence base is `references/artist-canon.md` (ranked sources, verified rules, the corpus study behind International Art English); the humanness research is `references/research.md`. Arranged on the framework spine (`references/framework.md`). This register has four sub-registers that pull in opposite directions, so almost every rule below says which one it means: (statement) the artist's first-person statement, (curatorial) the curator's third-person thesis, (wall) the label a visitor reads standing up, (essay) the catalogue or critical essay.

One thing to settle first. This register has a famous failure mode. Rule and Levine's 2012 corpus study named it International Art English: the e-flux press-release dialect where "the real" runs 179 times more often than in ordinary English, every adjective becomes a noun in -ity, agency dissolves into "serves to" and "seems to", and the point is recognition by insiders rather than being understood. That dialect is real and you should be able to recognize it. It is not the target. The target is the register written well: theoretically literate, thesis-driven, every abstraction paid for by something a reader can see. The good pole has names, Gilda Williams and Sarah Thornton and Peter Schjeldahl and Dave Hickey. Write toward them. The house core already bans most of what makes IAE read as parody.

## word stock

- Replace every "explores", "interrogates", "investigates", "negotiates", "engages with", "problematizes", and "questions" with a verb that names what the work literally does. These six verbs are the register's tell; a statement built on them asserts nothing.
- (statement, wall) Name the medium and materials in plain nouns: oil on canvas, non-narrative video, a durational performance. Not "lens-based practice" where "photographs" is true.
- Treat the abstract nominalizations as defects to be earned back: temporality, liminality, materiality, spatiality, "the visual" as a noun. Each one you keep, you keep because no concrete word carries it, and you anchor it to something in the room within the same sentence.
- (essay, curatorial) The theory lexicon is available and sometimes right: aporia, rupture, autonomy, biopolitical, the dialectical. Price each term. One unglossed theory word per paragraph is a strong voice; three is a smell. If a term is suddenly everywhere this season (speculative in 2009, rupture in 2011), it is signaling membership rather than meaning, so reach past it.
- (wall) A specialist term is allowed only when the object needs it, and it gets defined in the same sentence: "electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver". Ban the criticism words outright here: trope, iconography, emblematic, recontextualization.
- (essay) One precise adjective beats a stack of vague ones, and beats a balanced pair even more. "Playfully and subversively" is two words doing half a word's work; pick the one you mean.
- Prefer the short Saxon word to the Latinate: buy not purchase, watch not observe, show not present. The exception earns itself in essay register, where a Latinate abstraction is occasionally the exact tool.
- (curatorial, essay) Reuse the exhibition's key term on purpose as the spine of the text. Pedrosa states "Foreigners Everywhere" once and turns it through the literal, the psychological, and the political across the whole statement. That is what repetition is for, and it reads nothing like a thesaurus running dry.
- Banned: the formula openers ("my work explores", "my practice examines", "raises questions about", "blurs the boundaries between"); the stock closers ("invites the viewer to reflect", "questions dominant narratives"); value-adjective puffery (stunning, sensitive, powerful); and the whole AI-marker family from the universal core.

## pace

- Put the action in the first clause. The single most corpus-documented IAE move is opening with a stacked dependent clause that buries the verb ("Through an expansive practice that spans..."), which the essay calls an uncanny stillness. Subject, verb, object. Then complicate.
- (wall) First sentence under 16 words, carrying the most interesting thing you know. The visitor gives the object under 30 seconds, so the conclusion comes first, journalism-style, never built to.
- (statement) 100 to 300 words, one page the hard ceiling. Present tense for what the work does, past for how it came to be. Three-paragraph arc: the premise, the range with named works, the wider stake. Close on one sentence naming what you need remembered.
- (curatorial) 200 to 400 words for a wall or a website, up to 800 for a grant or catalogue, and cut toward the floor. One big idea; derive the labels and the press release from it.
- (essay) Sentences average in the mid-twenties, with the high-twenties as a ceiling and not a target, around 1.5 clauses each. Vary hard anyway: a five-word sentence against a forty-word one. Structure gets decided before style.
- (wall) One idea per sentence, one subject per paragraph, short paragraphs a standing reader can scan. Split anything over about 100 words into 50-word chunks placed at their own objects; the measured jump in who actually reads is large.
- Budget your ideas the way you budget words: one per wall label, two per statement, at most three per essay or press release.

## fillers

- (statement) Ban the openers by name: "my work explores", "my practice examines", "at the intersection of", "both X and Y", "ranges from". They are the discourse-cliché layer, and a reader who reads statements for a living skims straight past them.
- (essay) Stance and engagement markers are the register's connective tissue: "of course", "as we know", "arguably", the direct appeal to the reader. Soft-field academic prose runs about one interactional item every 28 words. A whole paragraph with none reads as a lab report rather than art writing. Use them; do not stack them.
- (wall) Strip all of it. No "of course", no shared-ground wink, no throat-clearing, no restated title. And check you have not started every label with "This cup", "This pot".
- Cut the paired modifiers and the hedged flourishes: "playfully and subversively invert", "internal psychology and external reality", "playful yet serious". One term always survives the pair; delete the other.
- (essay, curatorial) The translated-French connectives (simultaneously, while also, always already, whilst) are pure IAE filler yoking clauses that plain English would cut. Recognize them. Use them only if the clause genuinely needs joining.

## assumed context

- (statement) Front-load the basic idea in the first two or three sentences; a grant panel gives the whole application about eight minutes. Cite theory only where it bears on the day-to-day of the studio, and cut the rest. Describe process only when it is genuinely unusual and the images cannot show it; collage is not a unique process.
- (wall) Write for a bright reader with no subject knowledge, roughly an 8th-grade level, without thinning the content. Most museum visitors have no art training and many are not first-language English readers. Start from what is visible ("the ivy-swathed pedestal supports a male bust"), then widen; a label that reads without sending the eye back to the object has failed.
- (wall) Answer the obvious question first: why the hole, why a mirror frame with no mirror, what it was for. Sketch in the background the reader lacks ("mirrors were an expensive novelty"), and name people by their role rather than their fame ("a leading French sculptor" instead of "the famous David d'Angers").
- (curatorial) Assume the juror knows nothing of your background or methods and supply it in plain words. Make every document tell one story; a discipline named in the bio that never returns reads as a gap.
- (essay) You may assume a committed reader: open on biography, trace the turning points, synthesize. But never require the reader to hold a theory before the work becomes visible, and gloss any unfamiliar thinker in passing. The IAE move is the opposite, dropping "biopolitical" and "negation" unglossed because the register wants recognition over comprehension; know that you are choosing the reader when you do it.
- (essay, curatorial) Every text should answer three questions in some form, in this order: what is it (concrete description), what might it mean (interpretation), why does it matter (stake). Williams's rule. Do job one first.

## opinion and certainty

- (curatorial) State the exhibition's thesis as a claim rather than a musing. The curatorial voice is analytical and persuasive. Keep confession out of it. Hedge at the level of works ("most of these depict", "often") and commit flat on the thesis ("the primary focus is").
- (statement) Say why you make the work, what actually pulls at you rather than what you think a panel wants. No universal claims about your role in the world or what all viewers feel. Shorter reads as more confident; never pad to the limit.
- (essay) Commit to a judgment and own it. Hickey writes about how the work "seems to have worked in my experience" and refuses to prescribe what art must do; that is the model, a real verdict without false authority. Present interpretation as motivated possibility, substantiated by what is visible, never as the artist's private intent asserted as fact.
- Every hedge names its doubt. This is the house law and it is exactly where IAE fails: "serves to, functions to, seems to, might seem to" is agency dissolving on purpose so nothing can be pinned. If the work subverts, say it subverts. If you are unsure it does, say what you are unsure of.
- (wall) Interpret and provoke rather than instruct. Admit real uncertainty in plain words ("its history is a puzzle", "we assume", "probably") instead of scholarly fog. Never tell the viewer what to feel or what they can plainly see; if you ask a question, make it one with no easy answer.
- (essay) Hedge density sits high in this register, around 15 to 20 per thousand words, with boosters kept to roughly half that; caution outnumbers certainty in serious art writing. But every one of those hedges carries a real doubt. None is there for decoration.

## relationship

- (statement) First person throughout. Never "the artist". Pitch the register between an academic paper and a newspaper article. Read it aloud to a non-artist; if they lose the thread, rewrite. Typos read as carelessness to a juror.
- (curatorial) Third person, no "I", unless you are the artist curating your own show. Reserve first person for a single deliberate moment of stake-claiming, the way Pedrosa lets one "I myself feel implicated" land.
- (wall) Warm, conversational, close to spoken rhythm, active voice with an institutional "we" where an agent is needed. Passive is the genre's default failure. Write as you would speak. Put a person in every text where honesty allows, a named individual or a sensory detail (the cup "once smelled of cherry brandy"); people connect with people.
- (essay) Authority without the lecture, a knowledgeable friend talking. Thornton reaches outsiders and insiders at once by entering on universal stakes (money, status, meaning) and letting the specialist detail ride on top. Jargon is a discourtesy rather than a credential. Keep the spotlight on the work and away from your own virtuosity.
- Know what the register buys and what it costs. Fluent IAE signals a university-trained sensibility to peers and shuts out the general visitor; its vagueness is sometimes commercially load-bearing, since muddying a work's meaning can protect its price. That is a real function of the language, documented, and not yours to pretend away. Choose your reader, then price every borrowed term against them.
- Break any rule here sooner than write something flat or barbarous. Orwell's last rule, and Hickey's whole license: when a flourish is true to the work, keep it.

## sample

Before (AI, full IAE): "Through a rigorously interdisciplinary practice spanning sculpture, video, and performance, the artist interrogates the liminal spaces between materiality and memory, problematizing normative temporalities while radically reconfiguring the viewer's relationship to the politics of the body."

After (statement): "I make sculptures out of things that were almost thrown away: bent nails, a chair with one leg gone, dryer lint pressed into brick shapes. I am interested in the moment just before something becomes garbage, when it still remembers being useful. Lately the work has moved off the plinth and onto the floor, where you have to step around it the way you would step around a spill. Most of it will not last. That is part of what it is about."
