# Artist Canon

The evidence base for the artist tone. Part 1 ranks the sources and says how each earned its place. Part 2 lists the rules that survived verification, organized by framework dimension. Downgraded claims carry their caveat where they appear, and the one finding that failed its citation check sits at the bottom.

The register has a split personality, and the canon has to hold both halves. One body of work is descriptive: the corpus studies that document how the academic art dialect actually behaves, above all the essay that named International Art English. The other is prescriptive: the craft manuals and museum guidelines that say how to write the register well enough to be read and funded. The descriptive sources tell you what the tells are. The prescriptive sources tell you which ones to keep. The tone writes toward the second pole.

## Part 1: The Canon

Ranked by how much each source teaches about the language itself, weighted by whether its claims were measured or believed. Sources that appeared under several lenses are merged into one entry.

**1. Alix Rule and David Levine, "International Art English," Triple Canopy no. 16 (2012).** The one corpus study of the register, and the reason the register has a name. The authors loaded thirteen years of e-flux press releases into Sketch Engine and compared them against the British National Corpus. Every headline number checks out verbatim against the essay: "the real" runs about 2,148 per million words in the art corpus against 12 in the BNC, roughly 179 times more often; "reality" runs 313.7 against 64.1, which the authors call four times; the generative move is nominalization in -ity (visual to visuality, global to globality, potential to potentiality), traced to October's 1970s translations of French and German theory. Fully verified against the primary text. The one boundary the critics pressed: the BNC is a general-usage British baseline, a contestable norm, and only the lexical counts are quantified while the syntax claims rest on hand-picked examples.

**2. Ken Hyland, "Stance and engagement: a model of interaction in academic discourse," Discourse Studies 7(2) (2005).** The quantitative backbone for essay register. Hyland's Table 2 gives interactional density per thousand words across eight disciplines, and every figure verifies against the published table. Hedges run 18.5 in philosophy and 18.0 in applied linguistics against 8.2 in mechanical engineering; boosters sit near half the hedge count; self-mention lands at 4 to 6 in the soft fields; reader reference reaches 11.0 in philosophy against 0.1 in biology; the whole corpus averages one stance-or-engagement item every 28 words. It tells you the register's real calibration, which is not decoration piled on but a measurable rate of hedging, self-mention, and appeal.

**3. Gilda Williams, *How to Write About Contemporary Art* (Thames and Hudson, 2014).** The craft canon, and the strongest voice for the good pole. Her three-jobs rule is the test of any art text: say what it is, what it might mean, and why it matters, in that order. Her diction rules are the anti-IAE program stated plainly: load the text with solid nouns and strong active verbs, never explain a dense abstract idea with another dense abstract idea, one precise adjective over a stack of vague ones, drop the messy adverbs. She diagnoses the register's failure as fear, the inexperienced writer reaching for borrowed institutional fog. Asserted from long practice, and it operationalizes what the corpus studies imply.

**4. Lucy Trench, "Gallery Text at the V&A: A Ten Point Guide" (Victoria and Albert Museum, 2011).** The most prescriptive wall-text source, verified as real and correctly attributed. Ten points, each a worked rule: first sentence under 16 words where possible; the visitor gives an object under 30 seconds so the conclusion comes first; short Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ("the language of bureaucracy, dry and dead"); one idea per sentence and one subject per paragraph; admit uncertainty in plain words; put a person in every text because people connect with people. Institutional convention rather than experiment, but grounded in the museum's own visitor research and unmatched in word-level specificity.

**5. Beverly Serrell, *Exhibit Labels: An Interpretive Approach* (1996; 2nd ed. 2015) and "Paying Attention," Curator 40(2) (1997).** The measured floor under wall text. The 1997 meta-analysis pooled about 110 exhibitions across 62 museums and 8,507 visitors: roughly 80% of exhibitions were viewed in under 20 minutes total regardless of size, and the "diligent visitor" who stops at more than half the elements is her definitional threshold, not a finding. *Exhibit Labels* supplies the Big Idea doctrine and the practitioner word-count bands (object labels 50 to 75 words, panels 150 to 250). Treat the dwell-time numbers as measured and the word bands as field convention.

**6. Anna Karina Kjeldsen and Matilde Nisbeth Jensen, "When Words of Wisdom are not Wise," Nordisk Museologi (2015).** The accessibility corpus, verified in full. Thirty-three texts across ten museums: excessive jargon, officialese, compound noun phrases, and passive voice are the documented accessibility killers, and nominalization is a general problem, worst when the nominalization is also a difficult word. Three of the four art museums in the sample wrote exceptionally long texts, 300 to 800-plus words, against a corpus-wide trend toward shorter. It is the empirical case that art wall texts specifically balloon, and why they should not.

**7. Cecilia Lazzeretti and Marina Bondi, "'A hypnotic viewing experience'," (2012), with Lazzeretti, *The Language of Museum Communication* (Palgrave, 2016).** The genre analysis of the exhibition press announcement, verified. Positive evaluation clusters in four semantic areas (novelty, quality, extensiveness, exclusiveness) plus emotional language for news value; the announcement is a hybrid informational-promotional genre that addresses journalists as customers. It names the curatorial-promotional register's actual engine.

**8. GYST-Ink ("Getting Your Sh*t Together") and The Creative Independent, artist-statement guidance.** The professional-practice canon for the statement, verified as accurately represented. First person, never "the artist"; 100 to 300 words, one page the ceiling; name your medium concretely; cut childhood and biography unless load-bearing; kill the "my work explores / interrogates / blurs boundaries" openers. GYST is the standard US art-school professional-practices curriculum, founded by Karen Atkinson. Prescriptive, aimed at working artists, corroborated across CaFE, Queer|Art panel notes, and George Vasey.

**9. Hito Steyerl, "International Disco Latin" (e-flux journal #45, 2013) and Martha Rosler, "English and All That" (e-flux #45, 2013).** The primary rebuttals, and the reason the tone does not simply condemn the register. Steyerl concedes the statistics and refutes the norm: why should an art student in Skopje conform to the British National Corpus? Rosler adds the genre correction, that a press release is long-form advertising copy with embedded keywords, so mining it for a diagnosis mistakes the genre, and that nominalization and "space" are rife across all contemporary academic English since the spatial turn. Both are polemics with no counter-data, but their correctives are real and they keep the canon honest about whose standard is being enforced.

**10. Dave Hickey (*Air Guitar*, 1997), Sarah Thornton (*Seven Days in the Art World*, 2008), and Peter Schjeldahl.** The exemplars of critical voice at the good pole. Hickey commits to judgment in the first person and refuses to prescribe what art must do, writing about how work "seems to have worked in my experience." Thornton reaches outsiders and insiders at once by entering on universal stakes and letting specialist detail ride on top. Schjeldahl models economy, saying only exactly what needs saying in dense epigrammatic sentences. Asserted by career rather than measured, and the case for authority without the lecture.

**11. British Museum Interpretation Team, "Writing text for galleries and exhibitions" (2022).** The second institutional wall-text source, convergent with the V&A. Use dates not centuries, active voice with an institutional "we," avoid gender and cultural bias, break text into scannable paragraphs, start from what the visitor can see, answer the obvious question first. Its tracking data (visitors stop at 4 to 6 objects per room, most skip panels) is the behavioral case for the stand-alone label.

**12. Chao Lu et al., "Examining Scientific Writing Styles from the Perspective of Linguistic Complexity" (2018), and the ESP corpus of online exhibition overviews (2022).** The two sentence-and-length calibrations, both verified. Lu et al. put the mean sentence in published scientific articles at 26.9 to 28.2 words with 1.5 to 1.6 clauses, which sets the essay register's ceiling. The ESP corpus of 120 online painting-exhibition overviews from about 20 highly reputed UK and US museums averages roughly 293 words, which sets the curatorial overview's real length.

**13. George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946), and Tom Wolfe, *The Painted Word* (1975).** The two polemics, kept as polemics. Orwell's six rules are the root of the whole anti-abstraction program and reach the tone through the V&A's adoption of them. Wolfe's charge that modern art "exists only to illustrate the text" was panned in 1975 and rebutted by Krauss; it survives here as a named reader's suspicion to write against, the fear that the words have swallowed the work.

## Part 2: The Rules That Survived

### Word stock

- **Replace the six empty verbs (explores, interrogates, investigates, negotiates, engages with, questions) with a verb that names what the work does.** The register's clearest tell; a statement built on them asserts nothing. [Williams], [Creative Independent]
- **Treat abstract nominalizations as defects earned back one at a time.** Temporality, liminality, materiality, "the visual" as a noun. Rule and Levine identify -ity nominalization as IAE's generative engine; keep one only when no concrete word carries it, and anchor it in the same sentence. [Rule & Levine]
- **Price every theory term; one unglossed term per paragraph is a voice, three is a smell.** The register runs on a recognizable lexicon (aporia, rupture, autonomy, biopolitical, the dialectical), and its documented function is recognition by insiders. Reach past the season's fashionable token (speculative spiked in 2009, rupture in 2011). [Rule & Levine]
- **(wall) Allow a specialist term only when the object needs it, and define it in the same sentence.** "Electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver." Ban the criticism words outright: trope, iconography, emblematic. [V&A], [Kjeldsen & Jensen]
- **Prefer short Anglo-Saxon words to Latinate ones.** Buy not purchase, watch not observe. The V&A calls Latinate diction the language of bureaucracy; Orwell's rule two. [V&A], [Orwell]
- **(statement, wall) Name the medium and materials in plain nouns,** and give sensory concreteness: what the work looks, feels, smells, or sounds like. [Creative Independent], [Vasey]
- **(curatorial, essay) Reuse the exhibition's key term as the spine of the text** rather than cycling synonyms. Pedrosa turns "Foreigners Everywhere" through several registers across one statement. [Pedrosa 2024]

### Pace

- **Put the action in the first clause.** IAE's most documented syntactic move is opening with stacked dependent clauses that bury the verb, producing what the essay calls an uncanny stillness. Subject, verb, object, then complicate. [Rule & Levine]
- **(wall) First sentence under 16 words, conclusion first.** The visitor gives an object under 30 seconds, so the payoff opens the label, journalism-style. [V&A], [Serrell]
- **(statement) 100 to 300 words, one page the ceiling.** Present tense for what the work does, past for how it came to be; three-paragraph arc (premise, range with named works, wider stake); close on one sentence. [GYST], [Creative Independent]
- **(curatorial) 200 to 400 words for a wall or website, up to 800 for grant or catalogue, cut toward the floor.** The observed mean for online exhibition overviews at reputed museums is about 293 words. [ESP corpus], [Brandstory]
- **(essay) Mean sentence in the mid-twenties, high-twenties as a ceiling, about 1.5 clauses each; vary hard around it.** Calibrated off published scientific prose (26.9 to 28.2 words), so treat it as a ceiling. [Lu et al.]
- **(wall) One idea per sentence, one subject per paragraph; split anything over ~100 words into 50-word chunks at their own objects.** Dividing a long label into short ones raises the share of visitors who read; the direction is well established. [V&A], [British Museum], [Serrell]
- **Budget ideas: one per wall label, two per statement, at most three per essay or press release.** [Artinfoland], [Serrell]

### Fillers

- **(statement) Ban the formula openers by name.** "My work explores," "my practice examines," "at the intersection of," "both X and Y," "ranges from." Panel readers skim straight past them. [Vasey], [Creative Independent]
- **(essay) Carry stance and engagement markers at roughly one interactional item every 28 words.** "Of course," "arguably," "as we know," the direct appeal to the reader. A whole paragraph with none reads as a lab report. [Hyland 2005]
- **(wall) Strip all of it.** No shared-ground wink, no throat-clearing, no restated title; check you have not started every label with "This cup," "This pot." [British Museum]
- **Cut the paired modifiers and hedged flourishes.** "Playfully and subversively," "internal psychology and external reality," "invites the viewer to reflect." One term always survives the pair. [Rule & Levine], [Artinfoland]
- **(essay, curatorial) Recognize the translated-French connectives as filler.** Simultaneously, while also, always already, whilst. Use them only where the clause genuinely needs joining. [Rule & Levine]

### Assumed context

- **Every text answers three questions in order: what is it, what might it mean, why does it matter.** Do the concrete description before any interpretation. Williams's three-jobs rule, the tone's backbone. [Williams]
- **(wall) Write for a bright reader with no subject knowledge, roughly 8th-grade level, without thinning content.** Most museum visitors have no art training and many read English as a second language; the fault of bad accessible text is thin content, not plain language. [V&A], [Kjeldsen & Jensen], [Serrell]
- **(wall) Start from what is visible, then widen; answer the obvious question first.** A label that reads without sending the eye back to the object has failed. Name people by role rather than fame. [British Museum], [V&A]
- **(statement) Front-load the basic idea in the first two or three sentences.** A grant panel gives the whole application minutes. Cite theory only where it bears on the studio; describe process only when it is genuinely unusual. [Queer|Art], [Gilsdorf/Creative Independent]
- **(essay) You may assume a committed reader, but never require a theory before the work becomes visible.** Gloss any unfamiliar thinker in passing. Dropping "biopolitical" unglossed is the recognition register; know you are choosing your reader when you do it. [Porges], [Rule & Levine]
- **(curatorial) Assume the juror knows nothing of your background; make every document tell one story.** A discipline named in the bio that never returns reads as a gap. [Queer|Art], [CARFAC]

### Opinion and certainty

- **(curatorial) State the thesis as a claim rather than a musing; hedge at the level of works, commit on the thesis.** The curatorial voice is analytical and persuasive. Pedrosa qualifies the works ("most depict," "often") and commits flat on the show's focus. [Brandstory], [Pedrosa]
- **(essay) Hedge at 15 to 20 per thousand words with boosters near half that.** Caution outnumbers certainty in serious art writing; philosophy runs 18.5 hedges against 9.7 boosters. Every hedge carries a real doubt. [Hyland 2005]
- **(essay) Commit to a judgment and own it in the first person, claiming only what the work did in your experience.** Hickey's model, a real verdict without prescriptive authority. [Hickey]
- **Every hedge names its doubt.** This is where IAE fails: "serves to, functions to, seems to, might seem to" is agency dissolving so nothing can be pinned. If the work subverts, say so; if you doubt it, say what you doubt. [house core], [Rule & Levine]
- **(wall) Interpret and provoke rather than instruct; admit uncertainty in plain words.** "Its history is a puzzle," "we assume." Never tell the viewer what to feel or what they can plainly see; make any question a genuine one. [V&A], [Serrell]
- **(statement) Say why you make the work; make no universal claims about your role in the world.** Shorter reads as more confident; never pad to the limit. [GYST], [Queer|Art]

### Relationship

- **(statement) First person throughout, never "the artist"; pitch between an academic paper and a newspaper article.** Read it aloud to a non-artist; typos read as carelessness to a juror. [GYST], [CaFE], [RISD]
- **(curatorial) Third person, no "I," unless you are the artist curating your own show.** Reserve first person for one deliberate stake-claiming moment (Pedrosa's "I myself feel implicated"). [RISD], [Brandstory], [Pedrosa]
- **(wall) Warm, close to spoken rhythm, active voice with an institutional "we"; passive is the genre's default failure.** Put a person in every text where honesty allows, a named individual or a sensory detail. [V&A], [British Museum], [Kjeldsen & Jensen]
- **(essay) Authority without the lecture; enter on universal stakes and let specialist detail ride on top.** Jargon is a discourtesy rather than a credential. Keep the spotlight on the work. [Thornton], [Hickey], [Porges]
- **Know what the register buys and costs.** Fluent IAE signals a university-trained sensibility to peers and shuts out the general visitor; its vagueness can be commercially load-bearing, since muddying a work's meaning helps hold its price. Choose your reader, then price every borrowed term. [Rule & Levine], [Steyerl], [Beckett]
- **Break any rule sooner than write something flat or barbarous.** Orwell's sixth rule and Hickey's license: when a flourish is true to the work, keep it. [Orwell], [Hickey]

## Downgraded and corrected

- **The label-split reading figures ("11% to 28%") are not citable as measured.** The finding that dividing one 150-word label into three 50-word labels raises the share of visitors who read is genuine and well documented, but it traces to Bitgood, Nichols, Pierce, Conroy and Patterson (1986), the Anniston Museum mummy study, not to the Bitgood and Patterson 1993 journal article, and the specific percentages could not be confirmed in any accessible source. The 1993 paper reports reading rising to at most about 56% under the best conditions. Use the direction, never the numbers.
- **"The road to hell is paved with adverbs" is Stephen King, quoted by Williams,** not Williams's own line. The attribution travels with the quote; keep it straight.
- **The IAE syntax claims are examples, not counts.** Rule and Levine quantified the lexicon (word frequencies against the BNC) but illustrated the syntax (dependent-clause stacking, paired terms, double adverbs) with hand-picked passages. Rosler's rejoinder stands: nominalization and "space" are common to all academicized English since the spatial turn, so treat the syntactic tells as characteristic rather than proven exclusive to the register.
